EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

What actually drove instructor performance in September

Fourteen instructors taught 328 classes at Kwality House in September 2026, generating 2,090 check-ins at 45.4% capacity utilisation. On the surface Rohan Dahima "won" the month with 302 attendances — 14.4% of everything the studio delivered. This report tests whether that ranking survives scrutiny. It largely does not.

The single most important finding

After controlling for day of week, time of day, class format, room capacity and week of the month, only three of the fourteen instructors are statistically distinguishable from the roster average — and all three are distinguishable for being weak, not strong. Bret Saldanha (-1.56 attendees per class), Karanvir Bhatia (-1.29) and Raunak Khemuka (-3.49) carry genuine negative effects (p<0.05). The other eleven sit inside the noise band. The practical implication is that Kwality House's September result was determined far more by which slots existed and how they were formatted than by who stood at the front of the room — with one costly exception: the studio is carrying 70 classes a month on instructors who measurably under-deliver in them.

The five findings that should change decisions

1 · The instructor leaderboard is mostly a scheduling artefact — but the bottom of it is real
Observation
Raw average attendance per instructor ranges from 2.84 (Raunak Khemuka) to 8.44 (Reshma Sharma) — a 3× spread.
Evidence
A fixed-effects model on all 328 September classes (controlling for day, time band, format, log capacity and ISO week) explains 31.8% of attendance variance. Ten of the thirteen instructor coefficients are statistically indistinguishable from the roster average. Only three are significant and all three are negative: Raunak Khemuka −3.49, Bret Saldanha −1.56, Karanvir Bhatia −1.29.
Interpretation
Most of the apparent gap between instructors is the slot they were given, not the draw they exert. But three instructors lose roughly 1.3–3.5 attendees per class relative to what an average colleague would deliver in the identical slot.
Why it matters
Bret, Karanvir and Raunak together taught 75 of 328 classes (22.9%) and produced only 313 check-ins (15.0%). The measured deficit on those classes is roughly 147 attendances in one month.
Recommended action
Stop treating these three as interchangeable cover. Re-allocate their weakest recurring slots first (Monday 18:00 powerCycle, Monday 19:30 powerCycle, Saturday 16:30 powerCycle, Sunday 16:00 Barre 57) and redeploy to instructors with positive adjusted effects.
Expected outcome
Closing even half the measured gap on the 31 chronically sub-4.0 powerCycle classes would add ~75–95 monthly attendances.
Success metric
Roster-weighted average attendance per class ≥ 7.00 within two months, with no fall in total class count.
2 · Clients are slot-loyal, not instructor-loyal — which makes roster flexibility cheap
Observation
Instructor preference exists but is modest.
Evidence
Across the 474 clients who visited in September and attended two or more classes, the average share of a client's visits taken with their single most-attended instructor was 42.4%. A null model that randomly reassigns instructors while preserving each client's visit count and each instructor's overall popularity produces 35.7% — a lift of 1.19×.
Interpretation
Instructor affinity is real but accounts for only about one fifth of the concentration that a pure slot-driven schedule would not already produce. Clients book the 7:30 class; whoever teaches it is secondary.
Why it matters
This is the most commercially useful finding in the report: it means management can move instructors between slots — including removing weak ones — with far less demand risk than a "clients will follow the teacher" assumption would suggest.
Recommended action
Treat instructor changes as a low-risk lever. Validate with a controlled substitution protocol (see Report 04) rather than assuming lock-in.
Expected outcome
Greater freedom to optimise the roster on slot and format economics rather than on instructor politics.
Success metric
Client overlap in substituted slots stays within ±5pp of the 28.5% September baseline for unchanged slots.
3 · Rohan Dahima is the studio's load-bearing instructor — and that is a concentration risk, not a triumph
Observation
Rohan Dahima taught 40 classes (12.2% of the schedule) and drew 302 check-ins (14.4%).
Evidence
He is the only instructor who appears in the top three for every format he teaches: powerCycle 7.65 avg (vs 4.82 format average), Barre 8.19, Strength Lab 5.5. He also carries the highest load-adjusted resilience — his 40 classes span 14 distinct recurring slots and 17 separate days.
Interpretation
His headline number is partly volume (most classes) but his per-class performance is genuinely top-quartile in every format. He is doing the work of roughly 1.4 average instructors.
Why it matters
Instructor concentration is moderate in aggregate (Herfindahl 0.0851, effective N 11.75, top-3 share 34.7%), but the top three by attendance — Rohan, Pranjali Jain, Reshma Sharma — account for 34.7% of all check-ins. Losing any one of them to leave, illness or a competing studio would remove 8–14% of monthly attendance with no obvious internal replacement at that per-class level.
Recommended action
Build named understudies for Rohan's three highest-yield recurring slots (Wednesday 08:00 powerCycle, Tuesday 19:15 powerCycle, Thursday 18:00 powerCycle) and test them while he is still in place.
Expected outcome
Reduced single-point-of-failure risk; a measurable second-line bench.
Success metric
At least two other instructors each complete ≥4 classes in a Rohan-held slot at ≥85% of his trailing average, before any unplanned absence occurs.
4 · The instructors who "look" weakest on average are sometimes the best at hard slots
Observation
Anmol Sharma and Richard D'Costa have low total attendance but the highest average utilisation on the roster.
Evidence
Anmol Sharma: 14 classes, 61.6% utilisation — the highest of any instructor — because he is consistently assigned to small-capacity Strength Lab (capacity 7–10) rooms. His adjusted effect is 0.02, statistically indistinguishable from average.
Interpretation
Utilisation and total attendance answer different questions. Anmol fills small rooms; Cauveri Vikrant fills none (41.0%) because she is given 18–22 capacity barre rooms in soft slots.
Why it matters
Ranking on a single metric would have management demote Anmol and promote Cauveri. Both conclusions would be wrong.
Recommended action
Evaluate every instructor on schedule-adjusted effect, then on utilisation versus the capacity they were actually given, and only then on totals.
Expected outcome
Deployment decisions stop rewarding instructors who were simply handed better rooms.
Success metric
All instructor reviews cite the adjusted effect and the capacity-adjusted utilisation, not raw totals.
5 · New-client acquisition is the studio's weakest link and it sits with specific instructors
Observation
122 people took their first-ever Physique 57 class at Kwality House in September. Very few came back.
Evidence
Of those 122 first-timers, only 9.0% visited again within 21 days — the worst monthly conversion in the twelve months measured (June 2026 peaked at 25.7%). Cauveri Vikrant taught 36 of the 122 first-timers (29.5%) — but 30 of those arrived via hosted/corporate classes, so her apparent acquisition strength is an event artefact, not a conversion skill.
Interpretation
The studio is buying first visits through corporate hosted events and introductory offers, then failing to convert them. Hosted classes contributed 60 check-ins (2.9% of the month) at zero recorded revenue.
Why it matters
At a 9% 21-day conversion rate, acquisition spend is largely being wasted. Every percentage point of conversion is worth roughly 1.2 returning clients a month, compounding into the base.
Recommended action
Separate hosted-event attendance from organic trial in all reporting. Attach a named second-visit booking prompt to every first-timer, and measure conversion by the instructor who taught the first class.
Expected outcome
New-client 21-day return rate recovering from 9.0% toward the 18–20% achieved in March, May and June 2026.
Success metric
New-client 21-day return ≥15% for two consecutive months; hosted-class attendance reported separately from organic trial.

SECTION B

Schedule-adjusted performance: appeal or advantage?

A raw average conflates two things: how much demand an instructor personally attracts, and how much demand the slot they were handed already contains. To separate them we estimated an ordinary least squares model on all 328 September classes at Kwality House:

Attendance ~ instructor + day of week + time band + format + log(capacity) + ISO week

Day of week, time band and format enter as sum-to-zero contrasts; week controls for the studio-wide demand shift described in Report 02. Model R² = 0.318 on 328 observations. Instructor effects are then centred on the session-weighted roster average so they read as "attendees per class versus an average colleague in the same circumstances". Positive means the instructor out-draws the slot; negative means the slot was carrying them.

Reading this correctly

Eleven of fourteen instructors are statistically indistinguishable from one another once the schedule is held constant. That is not a null result - it is the finding. It means the roster is broadly interchangeable in quality and the variation management sees month to month is being produced by the timetable, not the talent. The actionable exception is a clearly identified bottom tier of three, where the effect is large enough to survive the controls and to be worth acting on.

Do clients follow teachers, or do they follow the clock? This matters directly: if clients are teacher-loyal, roster changes are dangerous; if they are slot-loyal, roster changes are one of the cheapest levers available.

Instructor changes are a lower-risk lever than management probably assumes
Observation
Clients who attend a recurring class do not stay with a single teacher once they visit more than twice.
Evidence
Zero of the 180 clients who attended four or more September classes used only one instructor. Of 55 two-visit clients, 12 (21.8%) were single-instructor. Observed top-instructor concentration of 42.4% is only 1.19× the 35.7% that a slot-driven schedule would generate by chance.
Interpretation
There is a real but modest instructor preference. It is not strong enough to make instructor redeployment commercially dangerous.
Why it matters
Management can optimise the roster on slot economics — moving people to where the demand is — instead of protecting incumbent assignments.
Recommended action
Run substitutions as controlled tests (Report 04 protocol) rather than avoiding them. Keep the slot and format constant and change only the instructor.
Expected outcome
A usable evidence base on instructor–slot fit within eight weeks.
Success metric

CRITICAL

What the headline numbers are not telling us

Six contradictions in the September instructor data

The instructor with the most attendance is not the best instructor
Rohan Dahima's 302 check-ins come from 40 classes — 12.2% of the schedule. Per class he delivers 7.55 against a studio average of 6.37, but his adjusted effect (+0.63, p not significant) says an average colleague in his slots would have delivered nearly as much. Reshma Sharma (25 classes, 8.44 avg, +0.35) and Vivaran Dhasmana (18 classes, 6.72 avg, +0.46) are doing more with less.
The highest utilisation on the roster belongs to one of its lowest-volume instructors
Anmol Sharma fills 61.6% of his capacity from just 85 check-ins, because his rooms seat 7–10. Cauveri Vikrant fills 41.0% from 141 check-ins because hers seat 18–22. Any ranking that mixes the two is measuring room size, not teaching.
The instructor with the most new clients converts almost none of them
Cauveri Vikrant taught 36 of the studio's 122 first-timers (29.5%) — the highest on the roster — but 30 of those arrived through hosted corporate classes. Her client return rate is 56.7%, the lowest on the roster, rising to 70.2% once hosted classes are stripped out. Reporting her as an acquisition leader would be wrong.
A strong average is hiding the studio's most volatile performer
Cauveri Vikrant's standard deviation across her 23 classes is 3.62 (CV 0.59) with a range of 1 to 18 — that 18 is a hosted corporate class. Richard D'Costa's CV is 0.81 across 8 classes. Volatility at this level means a single class tells you nothing, and neither does a single month.
Nobody is actually instructor-loyal above four visits
Zero of 180 clients with four or more September visits used a single instructor. The studio's repeat business is built on timetable habits and format preferences, not on teacher followings. Any strategy premised on teacher followings is premised on something the data does not show.
The bottom three are carrying a fifth of the schedule
Bret Saldanha, Karanvir Bhatia and Raunak Khemuka taught 75 classes (22.9%) for 313 check-ins (15.0%). Their combined measured deficit against a roster-average instructor in the same slots is roughly 147 attendances for the month — about 4.6% of total studio attendance, lost without a single cancelled class.

How to measure an instructor change

  • Change only the instructor. Hold day, time and format constant, or the result is uninterpretable.
  • Compare against same-day unchanged slots. In September that control was −1.65 attendees per class — large enough to reverse the sign of the conclusion.
  • Review the delta at eight weeks and the absolute level again at sixteen. A positive change into a structurally dead slot still leaves you with a dead slot.
  • Report the sample size. An instructor with eight classes cannot support a conclusion in either direction.

What would falsify this report

  • Bottom-tier instructors improving without any intervention would mean the September effects were noise, not signal.
  • Client return rates converging across instructors once hosted classes are excluded would remove the retention-based recommendations.
  • The adjusted-effect measure failing to predict the next eight substitutions, having predicted the last 23 at r = 0.69, would undermine Report 01 Section B and Report 04 Section D together.
  • New-client conversion remaining at 9% after two full months of the second-visit prompt would point to the offer or the market rather than the process.
METHODOLOGY

Methodology, definitions and data limitations

Sources and reconciliation

  • Primary attendance source: the Checkins sheet of "Day End Report - Part 4" (member-level, 151,999 rows across all locations; 66,626 at Kwality House).
  • Secondary source: "Day End Report - Part 3 - Sessions" (session-level, 9,782 Kwality House rows, Jan 2024 – Oct 2026), used for capacity, bookings, late cancellations, revenue and format.
  • Reconciliation: September 2026 session-level check-ins (2,090) equal member-level check-in records (2,090) exactly. Bookings (2,325) equal check-ins plus late cancellations (234) in 327 of 328 classes.
  • Corroboration: "Day End Report - Part 1 - Payroll" teacher-month aggregates were used to cross-check class counts per instructor.

Definitions

  • Attendance / check-in: a member-level record with Checked In = TRUE. Bookings and reservations are never counted as attendance.
  • Utilisation: check-ins ÷ session capacity, summed at the level of aggregation. Capacity differs by format and by room (5–22), so utilisation and attendance answer different questions.
  • Late cancellation: Is Late Cancelled = TRUE. Net bookings = check-ins + late cancellations.
  • New client: Class No = 1, i.e. the member's first-ever recorded class.
  • Repeat client: a member with two or more check-ins with the same instructor in September.
  • Time bands: Early ≤07:45; Morning 08:00–09:45; Late morning 10:00–11:30; Midday 12:00–13:00; Afternoon 16:00–16:45; Evening 17:00–18:30; Late evening 18:45+.

Statistical approach

  • Fixed-effects model: OLS, 328 observations, instructor + day-of-week + time band + format + log(capacity) + ISO week. R² = 0.318. Instructor coefficients re-centred on the session-weighted roster average.
  • Why OLS and not a raw comparison: instructors are not randomly assigned to slots. Without controls, an instructor's average measures their timetable as much as their draw.
  • Head-to-head: restricted to recurring slots taught by ≥2 instructors with ≥2 classes each between March and September 2026, giving direct within-slot comparison with no modelling.
  • Affinity null model: 200 Monte Carlo reshuffles of instructor labels within each client's own visit history.

Limitations — read before acting

  • No-shows are not observable. The data records net bookings (check-ins plus late cancellations). Members who booked and simply did not arrive, and members who cancelled outside the late-cancellation window, do not appear. Late-cancellation rate is therefore a partial proxy for booking friction.
  • Waitlist and turned-away demand are not recorded. Where a class is full, we can see it is full but not how many members wanted a place. The Strength Lab opportunity in Report 02 is therefore a floor, not a ceiling.
  • Retention windows are truncated. Check-in data extends to 2026-10-21, so the post-September observation window is 24 days, not 30. All month-on-month retention comparisons in this report use a consistent 21-day window for this reason.
  • Small samples. Richard D'Costa (8 classes) and Anmol Sharma (14) cannot support strong conclusions; their confidence labels reflect this.
  • September contained an 11-day festival and a full closure day. See Report 02 for the decomposition. Instructor comparisons in this report control for week but not for festival phase; treat single-month instructor movements with appropriate caution.
  • Instructor effect is not the same as instructor quality in the presence of unobserved slot characteristics. Where a slot has been held by one instructor for a year, the model cannot fully separate the two.
