EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

September in context: a growth streak that stopped

Kwality House ran 328 classes across 29 operating days in September 2026 and recorded 2,090 check-ins at 45.4% capacity utilisation. Those are respectable numbers. They are also, on closer inspection, the moment a nine-month growth run came to a halt — and the cause is specific, measurable and fixable.

The finding that reframes the month

September 2026 produced 2,090 check-ins. September 2025 produced 2,110. After twelve months of growth the studio delivered one per cent less than the same month last year. August 2026, by contrast, was 46% ahead of August 2025 (2,593 vs 1,776). This is not a seasonal blip that averages out, and it is not explained by the Ganeshotsav festival alone — the decline began before the festival started. It is explained almost entirely by one format: powerCycle lost 148 check-ins year on year and 11.3 percentage points of utilisation, while Barre 57 gained 90 and Strength Lab gained 38. The studio did not have a bad September. It had a powerCycle September.

Year on year: where the growth went

Total check-ins by format, September 2025 versus September 2026. Barre and Strength Lab both grew; powerCycle fell by 26%.

Month on month: every format fell

The five findings that should change the schedule

1 · powerCycle is in structural decline, not a September dip
Observation
powerCycle has fallen every month since mid-August and shows no sign of stabilising.
Evidence
Weekly average attendance per powerCycle class: 7.00 (week ending 16 Aug), 5.48, 5.19, 5.29, 4.76, 4.57, 4.72, 4.11 (week ending 4 Oct). Utilisation fell from 59.5% in June–August to 48.2% in September, and from 63.7% in September 2025. Three of the studio's four completely empty classes in September were powerCycle.
Interpretation
A decline that begins in mid-August, continues through a festival, and persists after it is not a calendar effect. Demand for this format is eroding.
Why it matters
powerCycle is still 26.5% of the schedule but only 20.0% of attendance. Eight of its twenty-one recurring slots average under 4.0 attendees.
Recommended action
Reduce powerCycle supply by roughly a third — cut the eight chronically sub-4.0 recurring slots first — and redeploy the floor time to Strength Lab and the high-yield Barre variants. Retain the five slots that work (Thursday 18:00, Tuesday 19:15, Wednesday 08:00, Wednesday 10:30, Saturday 10:00–11:30).
Expected outcome
Reallocating the 31 sub-4.0 powerCycle classes to formats at their own September averages is worth an estimated +128 to +151 check-ins per month on a like-for-like capacity basis.
Success metric
powerCycle utilisation back above 55% within two months on a reduced slot count; no recurring powerCycle slot below 4.0 average for six consecutive occurrences.
2 · Strength Lab is the studio’s only genuinely demand-constrained product — and it is capped at seven seats
Observation
Strength Lab runs at 82.4% utilisation with 40% of its classes completely full. The studio is turning people away and has no way to record it.
Evidence
57 classes, 338 check-ins, 410 places, 82.4% utilisation. 23 classes finished completely full and 41 reached 85% of capacity. In 18 of the 57 classes, net bookings exceeded room capacity. Utilisation has run at 82–83% for at least four consecutive months — this is a ceiling, not a peak.
Interpretation
At 82% utilisation with 40% of sessions sold out, marginal demand cannot be served. Every additional Strength Lab class at this fill rate adds roughly 5.8 check-ins that the studio currently cannot capture.
Why it matters
This is the only place in the schedule where adding supply demonstrably adds attendance rather than dividing it.
Recommended action
Add three Strength Lab classes per week in slots currently occupied by failing powerCycle classes: Monday 18:00, Thursday 10:30 and Wednesday 19:15. Install a waitlist so turned-away demand becomes measurable.
Expected outcome
Roughly +44 net check-ins per month after subtracting the attendance of the classes displaced, and a measurable waitlist within four weeks.
Success metric
Strength Lab utilisation sustained above 80% on the larger slot count, with waitlist volume recorded and reported weekly.
3 · The studio’s most-scheduled class is only its fourth best
Observation
Within the Barre family — 56% of the schedule — the allocation of class types does not match demonstrated demand.
Evidence
Studio Barre 57: 69 sessions at 6.43 average and 37.1% utilisation. Studio Mat 57: 22 sessions at 10.86 average and 54.3%. Studio FIT: 25 at 8.80 and 51.8%. Mat 57 draws 69% more people per class than Barre 57 from a third of the sessions.
Interpretation
Mat 57, FIT and Cardio Barre are under-scheduled relative to how clients actually vote. Barre 57 is the default filler.
Why it matters
Reallocating format within the same room, same instructor pool and same time slot is the cheapest change available — it requires no new capacity, no new hiring and no marketing.
Recommended action
Convert four weekly Studio Barre 57 classes in low-utilisation slots to Studio Mat 57 or Studio FIT, starting with Monday 07:15, Thursday 19:15, Tuesday 17:45 and Sunday 16:00.
Expected outcome
If conversion captures even 55% of the gap between Barre 57 and Mat 57 averages on four weekly classes, roughly +36 to +45 check-ins per month.
Success metric
Converted classes average ≥8.5 within six occurrences, and no neighbouring class in the same time slot falls by more than 1.5.
4 · Monday evening and Friday evening are structurally dead; Saturday late morning is structurally full
Observation
The weekly schedule is not aligned with where demand actually sits.
Evidence
Monday 17:00–18:30 averages 4.27 per class at 31.5% utilisation across 11 classes. Friday 18:45+ averages 3.17 at 27.5% utilisation across 6 classes — the worst cell in the studio, including one class with zero attendees. Saturday 10:00–11:30 averages 7.87 at 60.6% utilisation across 23 classes, the strongest window in the studio. Sunday runs only 8.0 classes per operating day against Monday’s 12.5.
Interpretation
Supply is concentrated on the days and hours with the weakest demand, and thin on the weekend late-morning window that demonstrably works.
Why it matters
Monday carries the highest daily class count (12.5) and the second-lowest average (5.80). That is 50 classes a month of below-average return.
Recommended action
Shift two to three classes per week out of Monday and Friday evenings into Saturday and Sunday late morning. Do not simply delete them — move them.
Expected outcome
Moved classes should deliver their new window’s average rather than their old one: a class worth 3.2 on Friday evening is worth roughly 7.9 on Saturday late morning.
Success metric
Saturday and Sunday late-morning utilisation sustained above 55% on the higher class count, and Monday evening utilisation above 40% on a reduced class count.
5 · Room capacity is mismatched to class demand on a third of the schedule
Observation
The same class type runs in rooms of 12, 20 and 22 seats depending on the slot, with no apparent relationship to how many people come.
Evidence
Studio Barre 57 ran at 12-seat capacity in six recurring slots and at 20–22 seats in six others. The 20–22 seat instances averaged 6.7; the 12-seat instances averaged 6.1 — essentially identical attendance in rooms of very different size, producing 31.9% versus 50.8% utilisation. Barre 57 as a whole fills 37.1% of the space it is given.
Interpretation
Attendance is set by demand for the slot, not by room size. Large rooms assigned to small classes create the appearance of terrible utilisation and consume space that high-demand formats could use.
Why it matters
Utilisation is the metric management is judged on, and a large share of the studio’s 54.6% unused capacity is a room-assignment artefact rather than a demand problem.
Recommended action
Establish a room-allocation rule: any recurring class averaging under 7 across four consecutive occurrences moves to the 12-seat room; the 20–22 seat rooms are reserved for Mat 57, FIT and Cardio Barre, which actually fill them.
Expected outcome
Reported utilisation rises without any change in attendance — this is a measurement correction — and the large rooms become available to formats that can use them.
Success metric
No recurring class in a 20+ seat room averaging below 7.0 for four consecutive occurrences; studio-reported utilisation recalculated on right-sized rooms.

What the phases show

Average attendance fell from 7.46 in the 1–13 August baseline to 6.64 in the pre-festival 1–13 September window — a drop of 0.82 before the festival had begun. It then fell a further 0.69 during Ganeshotsav itself, to 5.95, and recovered to 6.67 in the five days after it. Two separate things happened: a pre-festival softening, and a festival effect that the studio then recovered from.

Cross-studio validation

Over the same three phases, Supreme HQ Bandra went 6.14 (Aug) → 5.98 (1–13 Sep) → 4.71 (14–25 Sep) → 5.59 (26–30 Sep) and Kenkere House went 5.36 → 4.79 → 4.06 → 4.69. All three studios dipped during the festival and all three recovered after it. But in the pre-festival window, Kwality fell 0.82 while Bandra fell only 0.16. The festival was city-wide; the early-September softening was largely Kwality’s own.

Bookings versus actual attendance

Conversion and cancellation

Of 2,325 net bookings, 2,090 converted to attendance (89.9%) and 234 were late cancellations (10.1%). The late-cancellation rate is the lowest recorded in twelve months (September 2025: 27.0%), which is not good news — it reflects thinner booking pipelines rather than better behaviour.

Critical caveat: the source data records net bookings after early cancellations. Members who booked and did not attend, and members who cancelled outside the late window, are not observable in either file. True no-show rate cannot be calculated.

Format diagnosis

Barre 57 — stable, dominant, and internally mis-allocated
Observation
Barre 57 is 56.1% of the schedule and 63.8% of attendance. Its aggregate performance barely moved: 7.46 average in June–August versus 7.24 in September, utilisation 42.2% versus 40.1%.
Evidence
But the aggregate hides real dispersion. Within the family, Mat 57 averages 10.86 and FIT 8.80, while Back Body Blaze averages 4.11, Cardio Barre Express 5.33, Mat 57 Express 5.43 and Recovery 3.00. The spread between the best and worst Barre variant is 3.6×.
Interpretation
Barre as a category is not the problem. The mix inside it is: the studio schedules its weakest-performing variants 27 times a month (Back Body Blaze, Express formats, Recovery, Cardio Barre Plus) and its strongest — Mat 57 — only 22 times.
Why it matters
These variants use the same rooms, the same instructors and the same client base. Changing the mix is close to free.
Recommended action
Protect and expand Mat 57 and FIT. Consolidate Back Body Blaze, Recovery and the Express variants into fewer slots, and move the surviving slots to the 12-seat room.
Expected outcome
A higher average per Barre class without any increase in class count.
Success metric
Barre family average above 7.8 and Barre utilisation above 45% within two months, on the same or fewer classes.
powerCycle — the studio’s central problem
Observation
powerCycle accounts for 26.5% of classes and 20.0% of attendance, and it is deteriorating on every measure.
Evidence
Average 4.816091954022989 versus 5.947761194029851 in June–August and 6.370786516853933 in September 2025. Utilisation 48.2% versus 59.5% and 63.7%. Three of the four empty classes. Highest late-cancellation rate at 13.6%. Eight of twenty-one recurring slots average under 4.0.
Interpretation
Two things are happening at once. First, the format as a whole has lost roughly a quarter of its year-ago demand. Second, the slots that remain are heavily concentrated on instructors with the weakest measured effects — Bret Saldanha (26 powerCycle classes at 3.77) and Raunak Khemuka (19 at 2.84) hold 45 of the 87 classes between them.
Why it matters
This single format explains the entire year-on-year stagnation of the studio. Fix nothing else and fix this.
Recommended action
Do not defend the current slot count. (1) Immediately withdraw the three structurally dead slots — Monday 18:00 (0.75 avg), Saturday 16:30 (2.00) and Friday 10:00 (2.00). (2) Convert Thursday 10:30 (2.50), Friday 08:30 (2.67) and Wednesday 19:15 (4.00) to Strength Lab. (3) Re-instructor Tuesday 07:30 (3.00) with Vivaran Dhasmana on a six-week test. (4) Retain and protect the five slots that work.
Expected outcome
Roughly 31 classes a month move from a 2.4 average to an expected 5.5–6.5, worth +95 to +125 check-ins per month net of displacement.
Success metric
powerCycle utilisation above 55% on the reduced slot count within two months; no recurring powerCycle slot below 4.0 for six consecutive occurrences.
Strength Lab — saturated, and the only place where more supply means more attendance
Observation
Strength Lab runs at 82.4% utilisation — nearly double Barre — with 40% of classes sold out.
Evidence
57 classes, 338 check-ins on 410 places. 23 classes full, 41 at 85%+. Standard deviation of attendance is just 1.19 — the most predictable product the studio has. Utilisation has held between 82% and 83% for four consecutive months, indicating a hard ceiling rather than a peak.
Interpretation
At this fill rate with a 7-seat room, marginal demand is being refused. The studio has no waitlist field in either data source, so the true size of the lost demand is unknown — which is itself a reporting gap worth closing.
Why it matters
Every other recommendation in this report involves moving demand around. This is the only one that grows it.
Recommended action
Add three Strength Lab classes per week, and install a waitlist so refused demand becomes visible. The 82% utilisation is achieved with almost no marketing and no promotional pricing.
Expected outcome
+44 net check-ins per month from the three added weekly classes, plus a measurable waitlist within four weeks.
Success metric
Strength Lab utilisation stays above 80% on the enlarged slot count; waitlist entries recorded weekly and reviewed monthly.

SECTION E

Hidden inefficiencies

Do concurrent classes divide demand?

Of 269 distinct time slots in September, 213 ran a single class, 53 ran two and 3 ran three. Running two classes at once is associated with an average of 6.217 per class versus 6.441 for a single class — a dilution of 0.22 attendees per class, or about 3.5%. The dilution is real but small, and it is not a reason to avoid adding a class to a genuinely strong window. Where two concurrent classes both exceed 60% utilisation — Saturday 10:15, Tuesday 19:15 — the pair is capturing distinct demand, not splitting one pool.

Six inefficiencies the monthly report does not show

Weak formats are holding prime weekday-evening slots
Evidence
Monday 18:00 and 19:30 and Friday 19:00 and 19:15 are occupied by powerCycle and Cardio Barre Plus — four slots producing 4.75 attendees per class between them at 22% blended utilisation. Meanwhile Strength Lab, at 82% utilisation, has no Monday or Friday evening presence after 18:45.
Action
Reallocate: one Monday evening and one Friday evening slot to Strength Lab.
The strongest Barre variants are confined to mornings and Saturdays
Evidence
Mat 57 runs at 10.86 average but appears only once after 17:00 in the entire month (Sunday 17:15, 9.25 average — which works). FIT appears after 17:00 only on Monday (8.25). The studio’s best Barre products are barely offered in the evening band.
Action
Test one evening Mat 57 and one evening FIT slot on Monday or Thursday.
Sunday is under-served by 33% relative to Monday
Evidence
Sunday operates 8.0 classes per day against Monday's 12.5, yet Sunday's average attendance (6.84) beats Monday's (5.80) by 18%. Sunday late morning utilisation is 52.3% against Monday's 44.8%.
Action
Move two to three weekly classes from Monday to Sunday late morning.
Express and Recovery formats are consuming slots without earning them
Evidence
Cardio Barre Express (9 sessions, 5.33), Mat 57 Express (7, 5.43), Back Body Blaze Express (7, 6.71), PowerCycle Express (3, 4.67) and Recovery (4, 3.00) together account for 30 classes and 149 check-ins — 9.2% of the schedule for 7.1% of attendance, all at 15–47% utilisation.
Action
Consolidate to no more than three Express slots per week, in the 12-seat room.
Room sizes are assigned without reference to demonstrated demand
Evidence
Studio Barre 57 runs in 12-seat rooms in six recurring slots (6.1 average, 51% utilisation) and in 20–22 seat rooms in six others (6.7 average, 32% utilisation). Same class, same demand, double the wasted space.
Action
Adopt a right-sizing rule based on four-occurrence trailing averages.
Hosted corporate classes are inflating the headline
Evidence
Five hosted classes produced 60 check-ins (2.9% of the month) at zero recorded revenue and with a 21-day return rate far below the studio average. They are a marketing channel, not a retail product, and they distort every per-class metric they touch.
Action
Report hosted classes as a separate line in the monthly pack.
CRITICAL

What the headline numbers are not telling us

"2,090 check-ins" sounds like a normal month. It is a year of growth wiped out.
September 2025 produced 2,110 check-ins from 360 classes. September 2026 produced 2,090 from 328. Twelve months, 32 fewer classes, 20 fewer visits. August 2026 was 46% ahead of August 2025. The month-over-month slide from 2,593 to 2,090 is the story; the level is not.
"45.4% utilisation" is not 45.4% of anything meaningful.
Utilisation is computed on 4,606 places, but 410 of those places are in 7-seat Strength Lab rooms that run at 82% while 2,380 are in 18–20 seat Barre rooms that run at 37%. The blended figure describes the room mix, not the demand. 2,516 unused places is a headline; the actionable number is the 105 classes running below 35% utilisation.
"Barre is stable" is only true at the aggregate level.
Barre 57's average moved from 7.46 to 7.24 — but Mat 57 fell from 12.03 to 10.86, Thursday 19:15 Barre 57 fell from 10.92 to 5.75, and Tuesday 17:45 Barre 57 fell from 13.15 to 7.80. Two of the studio's historically strongest Barre classes lost roughly half their attendance. The stability is an average of gains and losses, not a steady state.
"powerCycle averages 4.82" hides that half of powerCycle works.
Thursday 18:00 powerCycle averages 8.75 at 88% utilisation; Wednesday 08:00 averages 7.60 at 76%; Tuesday 19:15 averages 7.60 at 76%. These are among the best classes in the studio. The format problem is concentrated in eight recurring slots — cutting powerCycle uniformly would destroy genuinely good classes.
"Only 4 empty classes" understates how close to empty many were.
4 classes had zero bookings, but 105 classes (32.0% of the schedule) ran below 35% utilisation and 23 ran at or below 20%. A 10-seat powerCycle with two people is commercially equivalent to empty but never appears in an empty-class count.
"Revenue per check-in of ₹910" is not evenly earned.
powerCycle generates ₹973 per check-in against Strength Lab’s ₹840 and Barre’s ₹908. powerCycle’s higher yield per head is real — which is exactly why cutting it must be selective, protecting the five productive slots and removing the eight dead ones.

METHODOLOGY

Methodology, definitions and data limitations

Sources and reconciliation

  • Checkins sheet ("Day End Report - Part 4", gid 0): 151,999 member-level booking records across all locations; 66,626 at Kwality House; 2,325 in September 2026.
  • Sessions extract ("Day End Report - Part 3 - Sessions"): 9,782 Kwality House session records, January 2024 to October 2026; 328 in September 2026.
  • Reconciliation: session-level check-ins (2,090) equal member-level records with Checked In = TRUE (2,090) exactly. Bookings (2,325) equal check-ins plus late cancellations (234) in 327 of 328 classes.
  • Four sessions had zero bookings and therefore no member-level records; they are retained in the session analysis as genuine empty classes, not treated as missing data.
  • Excluded: 33 placeholder records labelled "Unknown Class" (15 June – 13 August 2026, average attendance 0.1–0.4) that are system placeholders rather than real classes. None fall in September.

Definitions

  • Utilisation: check-ins ÷ the session’s own capacity, computed at the level of aggregation. Never a studio-wide capacity average.
  • Net bookings: check-ins + late cancellations, as recorded. Early cancellations are not present in either source.
  • Empty class: zero net bookings. Four in September.
  • Recurring slot: day + start time + format family. Strength Lab variants (Push, Pull, Full Body and the generic Strength Lab) are treated as one family because the studio consolidated them during August and September 2026 — see Report 04.
  • Time bands: Early ≤07:45; Morning 08:00–09:45; Late morning 10:00–11:30; Midday 12:00–13:00; Afternoon 16:00–16:45; Evening 17:00–18:30; Late evening 18:45+.
  • Operating day: a date on which at least one class ran. 29 in September; 25 September was a full closure.

Seasonal and calendar adjustments

  • Ganeshotsav ran 14–25 September 2026 (Ganesh Chaturthi 14 September; Anant Chaturdashi and visarjan 25 September). The studio recorded no sessions on 25 September.
  • September is therefore split into pre-festival (1–13), festival (14–25) and post-festival (26–30) phases throughout this report, with an August baseline for comparison.
  • Cross-studio comparison against Supreme HQ Bandra and Kenkere House is used to separate city-wide calendar effects from studio-specific ones.
  • September 2025 and September 2024 are used as seasonal comparators where the month-on-month comparison could be confounded by the festival.

Limitations — read before acting

  • No-shows are not observable. Neither source records members who booked and did not attend, or who cancelled outside the late-cancellation window. Late-cancellation rate is a partial proxy only.
  • Waitlist and refused demand are not recorded. Where a class is full we can see it is full but not how many were turned away. The Strength Lab opportunity is therefore a documented floor, not a ceiling.
  • Room identity is not available, only capacity. Room-allocation recommendations are inferred from capacity patterns; confirm against the actual room schedule before implementing.
  • October 2026 is incomplete in the source data and is used only for retention windows, never as a benchmark month.
  • Opportunity 1 (+385) is a statistical ceiling, not a forecast. It assumes demand materialises in classes that have been weak for months. It is included to size the prize, not to set a target.
  • Revenue figures come from the session extract; hosted classes show zero revenue and should not be read as generating none.
